Experimental Precautions & Scientific Reasons

ACCURACY 1. Add a few drops of oil to the holes in the block for the thermometer and the heater
Scientific Reason: Air is an extremely poor thermal conductor. Air pockets between the heater/thermometer and the metal block create high thermal contact resistance. Adding oil displaces the trapped air and ensures good thermal contact, enabling fast and efficient heat conduction from the heater to the aluminium block and from the block to the thermometer bulb.
SAFETY & ACCURACY 2. Completely insert the heating part of the heater into the block before switching on
Scientific Reason: To prevent the heater from overheating and burning out. If the heating element is exposed to air, heat cannot conduct away quickly. Furthermore, heat transferred directly to ambient air escapes without warming the block, causing electrical energy $\Delta E$ to be recorded while failing to raise the block's temperature ($\Delta T$), artificially inflating $c_{\text{exp}}$.
ACCURACY 3. Place the aluminium block on a wooden block
Scientific Reason: To prevent heat loss to the table via thermal conduction. Laboratory benches (especially metal, slate, or composite tops) act as large heat sinks with high thermal conductivity. Wood is a good thermal insulator that thermally isolates the block from the bench surface.
ACCURACY 4. Wrap the aluminium block with cotton wool
Scientific Reason: To prevent heat loss to the surroundings via convection and radiation. Cotton wool traps tiny pockets of still air, providing an insulating barrier that minimizes thermal energy escaping from the hot block surfaces into room air.
SAFETY 5. Do not touch the immersion heater or block immediately after heating
Scientific Reason: Beware of the hot heater! The immersion heater and block attain temperatures capable of causing severe skin burns. Always allow the equipment to cool naturally before touching or disassembling the apparatus.

Discussion Questions & Standard HKDSE Solutions

1. Why should we add some oil to the hole that contains the thermometer? +
Air is a poor conductor of heat. Without oil, the small air gap between the thermometer bulb and the metal wall creates large thermal contact resistance. Adding drops of oil expels the trapped air and ensures good thermal contact between the aluminium block and the thermometer, allowing the thermometer to respond rapidly and reach thermal equilibrium with the block.
2. Compare the experimental value with the standard value ($900\text{ J kg}^{-1}{^\circ\text{C}}^{-1}$). Account for any difference between the two. +
The experimental value is typically higher than the standard reference value ($900\text{ J kg}^{-1}{^\circ\text{C}}^{-1}$).

From the formula $c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m \cdot \Delta T}$:
  • Heat lost to the surroundings: Some supplied electrical energy $\Delta E$ escapes to the surrounding air via convection and radiation, and to the benchtop via conduction. As a result, the measured temperature rise $\Delta T$ is smaller than it would be in an ideal insulated system, leading to an overestimate of $c$.
  • Heat absorbed by heater and thermometer: A portion of the energy is consumed in heating up the immersion heater casing and the glass thermometer rather than the aluminium block itself.
3. Why does the thermometer reading continue to rise after the heater has been switched off? +
The heating element inside the heater is much hotter than the aluminium block when the power is turned off. Residual heat continues to conduct from the heating element into the aluminium block until they reach thermal equilibrium.

Why record the highest temperature? The joulemeter recorded all the electrical energy delivered to the heating element. Recording the highest temperature ($T_1$) ensures that all this stored thermal energy transferred into the block is fully accounted for.
4. Would overestimating the mass of the aluminium block explain why the experimental value is higher? +
No. In the formula $c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m\,\Delta T}$, the mass $m$ appears in the denominator. If the mass $m$ is overestimated, the calculated value of $c$ will become smaller, not larger. Therefore, mass overestimation cannot explain an experimental value that is higher than standard.
