Backward & Forward Error Analysis in Heat Experiments

Physics Problem-Solving Tutorial (Active Physics 2, Chapter 2) • Interactive Step-by-Step Training

Concept: Forward vs. Backward Analysis

Approach Starting Condition Target Goal Typical Framing
Forward Analysis A known experimental imperfection (e.g., balance not tared, heat loss). Determine how the calculated quantity will be shifted (overestimated vs. underestimated). "The stopwatch ran too fast. How does this affect calculated $c$?"
Backward Analysis An unexpected calculated outcome (e.g., calculated $c$ is higher than standard). Identify which physical errors or omissions could produce this discrepancy. "The calculated $c$ is $5000\text{ J kg}^{-1}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$ instead of $4200$. What caused this?"

The Universal 4-Step Method

Step 1: Write Equations

Write the governing thermal relationships: $E = mc\Delta T$, $E = Pt$.

Step 2: Change Subject

Rearrange equation to isolate target variable ($c = \frac{Pt}{m\Delta T}$ or $t = \frac{mc\Delta T}{P}$).

Step 3: Relate Variables

Numerator $\propto$ target (direct); Denominator $\propto \frac{1}{\text{target}}$ (inverse).

Step 4: Factor Analysis

Determine whether each real-life mistake increases/decreases the variables and form the conclusion.

Worked Example

Amy: Faulty Stopwatch

Amy has a liquid of mass $0.8\text{ kg}$. She uses an immersion heater of known rated power to heat up the liquid. The temperature rises by $10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ when $6000\text{ J}$ of energy is transferred to it. The specific heat capacity calculated is:

$$c = \frac{E}{m \cdot \Delta T} = \frac{6000}{(0.8)(10)} = 750\text{ J kg}^{-1}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$$

Suppose the stopwatch that Amy used is faulty, and the measured time is longer than the actual heating time. How does this error affect the calculated specific heat capacity?

Model 4-Step Solution:

Step 1: $E = mc\Delta T$ and $E = Pt$

Step 2: $c = \frac{Pt}{m \Delta T}$

Step 3: The inaccurately measured quantity is the time $t$.

Step 4: Since $t$ is in the numerator, $c \propto t$. As measured $t$ is larger than actual, the calculated $c$ is overestimated (higher). Consequently, the actual specific heat capacity is smaller.

Worked Example

Peter: Overestimated Specific Heat Capacity of Water

Peter heats $500\text{ g}$ of water using an immersion heater without lidding the cup. Energy transferred is $15\ 000\text{ J}$, and recorded $\Delta T = 6\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Peter switches off the heater and records the water temperature immediately.

$$c = \frac{15\ 000}{(0.5)(6)} = 5000\text{ J kg}^{-1}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \quad (\text{Standard: } 4200\text{ J kg}^{-1}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1})$$

Deduction: The calculated value is larger than standard. Account for the difference.

Backward Deduction Protocol:

• Step 1 & 2: $c = \frac{E}{m \cdot \Delta T}$.

• Step 3: Calculated $c$ is larger if: measured $E$ is larger, measured $m$ is smaller, OR measured $\Delta T$ is smaller.

• Step 4: Factor evaluation:

✓ Cup not lidded: Heat is lost to air $\implies$ heater must supply more energy for a given rise $\implies$ measured $E$ is larger $\implies c$ larger.

✓ Recorded temperature immediately: Convection not finished and heater still transferring residual heat $\implies$ measured $\Delta T$ is smaller than maximum $\implies c$ larger.

✗ Heater left in when measuring mass: Would make measured $m$ larger $\implies c$ smaller (rejected).
